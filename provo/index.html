How Drying Works on a Provo Water Damage Job

A wet building left alone does dry eventually, but far too slowly. Moisture inside walls, studs and subfloors can linger for weeks, and that is plenty of time for mold to grow and wood to distort. Our job on every Provo property is to speed that up in a controlled way.

First the liquid water comes out, because extraction removes far more water per hour than evaporation ever will. Next, air movers placed around the room drive air over damp materials so the water in them evaporates. Dehumidifiers pull that moisture out of the air and send it down a drain, which keeps the room dry enough for evaporation to continue. Air movers on their own only stir humid air, so the two machines work as a pair.

We take moisture readings every day and compare them with a dry part of the same building. For most Provo homes the target is met in 3 to 5 days, though hardwood, plaster and other dense materials need more time. We remove the machines when the readings reach the goal, and not before.

Simple Ways to Keep a Provo Home Dry

Houses tell you when water is getting where it should not. Our technicians see the same early clues again and again in Provo, and they are worth learning. Blistering paint, ceiling stains, warped baseboards and floorboards that curl at the edges each point to water behind or beneath them. A musty smell in a closet, a toilet that rocks, or a tap that drips belongs on the list too.

No amount of maintenance prevents every leak. What it does is make them rarer and smaller, and that matters when the alternative is days of drying equipment in your Provo living room.

Water Damage We See Most in Provo

Not every water problem announces itself with a flooded floor. Below are the situations our crews are called to most often around Provo, and what each one tends to involve.

Hidden leaks: A pinhole in a supply line or a loose drain fitting can drip inside a wall for months. Watch for a water bill that climbs for no reason, soft spots on the floor, and paint that blisters. Our technicians trace the wet area with moisture meters and thermal cameras, without tearing out more wall than needed. Left alone, constant dampness rots framing and feeds mold, so a minor drip is still worth fixing now.

Sewage: A blocked sewer line sends waste back into the house, usually at the lowest shower, toilet or floor drain. It is typically caused by roots in the line, built-up grease or a municipal sewer overwhelmed during a downpour. Sewage counts as Category 3, the most contaminated class, and it is not safe to clean up bare-handed. Porous items it touches, such as carpet and pad, generally have to be removed.

Storms: Hard rain can outrun gutters and yard drains, then pool against the house. From there it comes in under doors, through window wells and across garage floors. Floodwater from outside picks up soil, fertilizer and whatever else it crossed, so we treat it as contaminated. Homes across Provo can see this in any season with a strong enough storm.

Crawlspace: The space beneath a raised floor is easy to forget, which lets dampness go unchecked for years. Humidity stays high down there when a pipe drips, the ground cover is damaged or water pools after rain. Over time that leads to sagging insulation, musty odors indoors and soft or cupped flooring. We pump out what has collected, dry the joists and subfloor, and show you where the moisture is coming from.

Condensate: An air conditioner pulls gallons of water out of the air on a humid day. That water should leave through a small drain line, which clogs easily with algae and dust. When it backs up, the pan overflows into the ceiling, closet or hallway around the air handler. Most owners learn of it from a ceiling stain or drooping drywall, so have the drain line cleared once a year.
